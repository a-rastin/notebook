Algorithm 1

Emergency Evaluation of the Adult with New Nontraumatic Headache

Step sequentially or toggle any clinical question below. Abnormal neurologic exam findings remain the single best clinical predictor of intracranial pathology.

1

Does the patient report a sudden, severe "thunderclap" headache?

SAH (~8%) & RCVS

Severe, persistent headache reaching maximal intensity within a few seconds to minutes (or <1 hour) after onset. Contrast with migraine, which gradually builds over 1–2 hours.

Immediate Thunderclap Workup Protocol

Critical Action
  • Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH): Present in ~8% of ED thunderclap headaches. Look for syncope/near-syncope at onset, neck pain/meningismus, diplopia, retinal/subhyaloid hemorrhage, or CN III palsy (unilateral dilated poorly reactive pupil).
  • Reversible Cerebral Vasoconstriction Syndrome (RCVS): 2nd most common cause of thunderclap headache. Presence of ≥3 recurrent short thunderclap headaches over days to weeks strongly suggests RCVS. Common triggers: exertion, emotional stress, cough, pregnancy, vasoactive drugs, hot/cold exposure.
  • Other Thunderclap Etiologies: Cervical artery dissection, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, pituitary apoplexy, acute angle-closure glaucoma, unruptured aneurysm, colloid cyst of 3rd ventricle, hypertensive emergency.

Interactive 6-Hour CT Rule & Traumatic LP Resolver Sub-Tool

Action Required: Because CT was obtained ≥6 hours after headache onset (or timing is uncertain) and is normal, perform a Lumbar Puncture (LP). Measure opening pressure, RBC count in tubes 1–4, and xanthochromia.
How to Distinguish Traumatic LP vs. True SAH (Click to expand)
  1. Opening Pressure: >20 cm H2O suggests a pathologic process.
  2. RBC Clearing (Tubes 1 to 4): Clearing of blood is ONLY reliable if the late/final collecting tube RBC count approaches ZERO. Clinical Tip: Waste several mL of CSF between the first and last tubes (safe because 10 mL of CSF is produced every 20–30 minutes).
  3. Xanthochromia: Presence strongly suggests true SAH (though false positives can occur).
  4. Repeat LP Cephalad: Repeat LP one intervertebral level cephalad (no higher than L3/L4) or under fluoroscopic guidance; normal repeat CSF makes SAH less likely.
  5. Persistent Ambiguity: Obtain cerebrovascular imaging — Head MRI w/ & w/o contrast + Craniocervical MRA (preferred) OR Head CT w/ contrast + Craniocervical CTA, and consult Neurology/Neurosurgery.
2

Does the patient have symptoms suggestive of meningitis and/or encephalitis?

CNS Infection

Fever (>38°C), meningismus, acute rash (petechiae/purpura or erythema migrans), or confusion/lethargy (may be sole finding in older adults; meningismus is less sensitive >60 yrs; Lyme meningitis often lacks severe findings).

Immediate CNS Infection Sequence (Do Not Delay Antibiotics)

  1. Step A — STAT Blood Cultures: Draw two sets of blood cultures immediately prior to antimicrobials.
  2. Step B — Empiric Antimicrobials (+/- IV Dexamethasone): Institute immediately before imaging or LP.
    • Add IV Acyclovir if Herpes Simplex Encephalitis is a consideration (confusion, temporal lobe signs, seizures).
    • Add IV Doxycycline if tick-borne disease (Rocky Mountain spotted fever, anaplasmosis, Lyme) is suspected.
    • Consider fungal (cryptococcal) and tuberculous meningitis in immunocompromised hosts.
  3. Step C — Determine if Head CT is Mandatory Before LP: Use the 6-criteria checklist on the right. In the absence of these 6 criteria, do NOT delay LP to await a Head CT.

3

Is there clinical concern for Carbon Monoxide (CO) exposure?

Toxic Exposure

Drowsiness, occurrence in winter or following power outage, smoke/fume inhalation from engines or fuel-based heaters, multiple household members/coworkers sick simultaneously, or headache resolving rapidly in ED away from source.

Immediate Carbon Monoxide Toxicity Protocol

  • Immediate Therapy: Administer 100% High-Flow Oxygen via non-rebreather mask immediately.
  • Diagnostic Confirmation: Measure Carboxyhemoglobin concentration with Co-oximetry using a blood gas sample.
  • Severity Spectrum: Mild/moderate exposure causes malaise, nausea, dizziness; severe exposure causes coma, seizures, myocardial ischemia, and cherry-red macula on fundoscopy.
Critical Diagnostic Pitfall

Standard pulse oximetry (SpO2) CANNOT differentiate carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin (gives falsely normal readings). Furthermore, arterial PaO2 on ABG is normal because it measures dissolved oxygen in plasma, not hemoglobin-bound O2.

4

Does the patient have ocular symptoms or temporal artery abnormalities?

Sight-Threatening

Vision loss, eye pain, halos around light, blurred vision (possibly transient), red/tearing eye, temporal artery tenderness/nodularity, or jaw claudication.

Ophthalmic Emergency

Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma

Unilateral eye pain, red/tearing eye, decreased vision, halos, nausea/vomiting (can mimic migraine).

Exam: Red injected conjunctiva (ciliary flush), "steamy" edematous cornea, fixed mid-dilated pupil (4–6 mm), firm globe, IOP often >30 mmHg.

Do NOT delay treatment with unnecessary brain imaging!

Age ≥ 50 Years

Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA)

Patient ≥50 yrs with new headache (often temporal), abrupt visual disturbances (transient monocular vision loss), jaw claudication, fever.

Exam: Palpable, nodular, tender, or diminished-pulse temporal arteries; elevated ESR and CRP.

Elevated ICP

Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension (IIH)

Overweight/obese female of childbearing age; intermittent visual decline, flashes, diplopia, tinnitus.

Exam: Papilledema, CN VI palsy (abducens), visual field cut. Perform Brain MRI w/ & w/o contrast (+ MRV) BEFORE LP with opening pressure.

5

Does the patient have a new, unexplained neurologic deficit or papilledema?

Single Best Predictor

Hemiparesis, hemibody numbness, speech impairment/aphasia, Horner syndrome (ptosis, miosis), ataxia/gait impairment, pronator drift, visual field cut, CN palsies, or papilledema.

First Action Check Point-of-Care Fingerstick Glucose STAT: Hypoglycemia causes both altered mental status and focal neurologic deficits mimicking stroke.

Targeted Neurovascular & Brain Imaging Pathways

A. Neck Pain and/or Horner Syndrome → Suspect Cervical Artery Dissection

Horner syndrome (miosis, ptosis, anhidrosis) occurs in 38–47% of carotid and up to 19% of vertebral dissections. Only 3.2% (carotid) to 9% (vertebral) have thunderclap onset; ~60% have ischemia/infarction.

Workup: Noncontrast Head CT followed by Head & Neck CTA w/ IV contrast (or MRI/MRA w/ & w/o contrast) from the thoracic inlet to Circle of Willis.

B. Focal Deficit or Papilledema → Mass, Stroke, Abscess, Hydrocephalus

1st-Line: Head MRI without and with contrast (or Noncontrast Head CT initially if MRI not immediately available to exclude hemorrhage/hydrocephalus/mass effect).
Renal Impairment Rule (eGFR <30 & not on dialysis): MRI without contrast is preferred over CT without contrast.

Distinguishing Migraine Aura vs. Destructive Lesion UpToDate Table 5

Never assume a focal deficit is migraine aura unless identical deficits occurred with prior migraines.

Positive Phenomena (Excitatory)

Suggests Migraine Aura or Seizure (gradual spread over minutes):

  • Visual: Scintillations, flashing lights, zigzag lines (fortification spectra)
  • Somatosensory: Paresthesias (pins and needles, tingling)
Negative Phenomena (Loss of Function)

Suggests Destructive Lesion (Stroke, Hemorrhage, Mass):

  • Visual: Complete loss of vision, dark/black visual field cut
  • Somatosensory: Complete numbness / loss of sensation
  • Motor: True weakness, hemiparesis, paralysis, aphasia
6

Is the patient pregnant (≥20 weeks) or postpartum (up to 6 weeks)?

Preeclampsia / CVT / PRES

Preeclampsia/eclampsia must be considered in every pregnant woman ≥20 weeks gestation and up to 6 weeks postpartum.

Pregnancy & Postpartum Secondary Headache Differential & Workup

1. Preeclampsia / Eclampsia & PRES

Most common dangerous cause. Presents with hypertension, proteinuria, visual symptoms, hyperreflexia, or seizure up to 6 weeks postpartum. Overlaps with PRES.

2. Cerebral Venous Thrombosis (CVT)

Especially common in the postpartum hypercoagulable state. Variable headache, seizures, papilledema, or non-arterial focal deficits. Also consider Pituitary Apoplexy & RCVS.

3. Postdural Puncture / Intracranial Hypotension

Headache worse within 15 min of standing and relieved lying flat (from spinal/epidural anesthesia OR spontaneous dural tear during labor pushing).

Discharge Counseling

Medication Overuse Headache (MOH) Risk Stratification

Educate all ED headache patients at discharge to prevent chronic rebound headache.

HIGHEST RISK FOR MOH Avoid / Strictly Limit

Opioids, Butalbital-containing combination analgesics, and Aspirin-Acetaminophen-Caffeine combinations.

INTERMEDIATE TO HIGH RISK Limit < 10 Days / Month

All Triptans (sumatriptan, eletriptan, rizatriptan, zolmitriptan, etc.) and Ergotamine preparations. Must be limited to fewer than 10 days per month.

LOWEST RISK (POTENTIALLY PROTECTIVE) Limit < 15 Days / Month

Simple NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, ketorolac) and Acetaminophen. Limit to <15 days per month. NSAIDs may actually protect against chronic migraine in patients with <10 headache days/month.

Pharmacovigilance

Cardiac ECG Monitoring & Drug Interaction Rules

Critical safety checkpoints for parenteral antiemetics, triptans, and cluster prophylaxis.

1. QT-Prolonging Antiemetics (Chlorpromazine, Haloperidol, Droperidol, Ondansetron)

Can cause dose-dependent QTc prolongation and torsades de pointes. Avoid if congenital Long QT or persistent QTc >500 ms. If used, obtain ECG at baseline and 2–3 hours post-dosing. Note: Ondansetron & Granisetron cause headache as an adverse effect; guidelines recommend against IV granisetron for migraine.

2. Triptan Washout & Drug Interactions
  • 24-Hour Rule: Never administer a triptan within 24 hours of ergotamine/DHE or a different triptan.
  • Propranolol + Rizatriptan: Propranolol increases rizatriptan levels by 70% → reduce rizatriptan dose (use 5 mg).
  • CYP3A4 Inhibitors + Eletriptan / DHE: Do not use eletriptan within 72 hours of potent CYP3A4 inhibitors (ketoconazole, itraconazole, clarithromycin, ritonavir).
  • SSRIs / SNRIs + Triptans: Added risk of serotonin syndrome is very low to nonexistent; may co-prescribe if needed with patient counseling and monitoring. Avoid MAOIs.
3. Cluster Headache Verapamil & Lithium Monitoring

Verapamil: Obtain pretreatment ECG and mandatory ECG after every dose increment above 480 mg/day (up to 960 mg/day) to screen for heart block/bradycardia.
Lithium Carbonate: Target plasma level 0.6–0.8 mEq/L (max 1.2 mEq/L); monitor baseline ECG (if >40 yrs or CV risk), thyroid, renal, and liver function.
